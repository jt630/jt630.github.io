The one thing that matters most: the CVT fluid. Subaru's "Normal" schedule calls it lifetime; their own "Severe" schedule (which covers exactly the driving this car will see — Boise gravel, mountain trips, short commutes) says every 30,000 miles. There's no record of it ever being changed. At 35k miles, do it now — it's the cheapest insurance against the one failure mode that actually ends a Subaru.

The min-max checklist — first month

Coming from a 2006 car, a lot of this generation's stuff didn't exist yet. None of it is hard, all of it is worth doing in the first few weeks rather than discovering it by accident.

Do this week

  • Check for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls by VIN ({{ $h.vin }}) — free, 30 seconds, and CarMax's own check only covers what's been reported to them.
  • Get a spare key cut now. Used cars often come with one fob. Losing your only key is a $300–400 dealer/locksmith job and you can't drive the car in the meantime. A Subaru dealer or a locksmith with the right equipment can cut and program a second one for less if you do it before you need it.
  • Transfer the MySubaru / Starlink account. Subaru renamed Starlink to MySubaru Safety/Security; it's tied to the VIN, not the car. Call Subaru with the VIN + your bill of sale — they detach it from the previous owner (occasionally needs a notarized affidavit if that owner is unreachable) and let you re-enroll. Do this early: remote lock/start and recall/service alerts route through it. Note that the original owner's free trial (3yr Safety / 6mo Security from new) is long expired on a 2019 car — you'll be buying a subscription, not getting a fresh trial.
  • Delete the previous owner's paired phone from Bluetooth before pairing your own — it's very likely still paired and may still be routing call/text notifications through the car.
  • Download the digital owner's manual from Subaru's site by VIN or model year — a used car frequently doesn't come with the glovebox copy.
  • Confirm full-coverage insurance is bound before you drive it off the lot; your lender requires it and CarMax won't release the car without proof.
  • Save the roadside assistance number into your phone now, not when you need it on the side of a mountain road.

Make it yours

  • All-weather floor liners (genuine Subaru or WeatherTech, by year/trim) — protects a genuinely clean interior through Boise winters and gravel-road mud.
  • Check the mirror for HomeLink before assuming you have it. Subaru's own equipment pages don't cleanly confirm whether HomeLink was standard on 2019 Limited or Premium-package-only, and owner reports disagree. Look at the bottom of the rearview mirror housing: three unlabeled buttons means the hardware's there and just needs pairing to your garage door; a plain mirror means it wasn't installed and can't be retrofitted without swapping the whole mirror assembly.
  • Set the infotainment preferences: clock/date (this unit usually pulls time from GPS once nav gets a fix — check it indoors before that happens), backup-camera guideline lines on/off, auto lock-on-drive and unlock-on-park behavior — all buried in a settings menu that didn't exist in 2006.
  • Update the nav/map software the real way for this generation — it's USB-based, not over-the-air: on the head unit go to Settings → Navigation → Map Update Information to get a request code → create a free account at subaru-maps.com → enter the code for an activation code → install Subaru's "Map Downloader" app on a computer → download the update to a 16GB+ USB drive → plug into the front USB port with the engine running outdoors (never in a closed garage) and follow the prompts.
  • Look for a memory-seat button on the door armrest (numbered "1"/"2" near the window switches) before assuming you have seat-position-per-key-fob — two independent sources say that feature was Touring-exclusive in 2019, not included on the Limited. If the button's not there, this car doesn't have it, no matter what the generic owner's manual describes for the model line.

Know before you need it

  • There's a hidden keyless-entry PIN — this is the "trunk code" you were thinking of. There's no numeric keypad mounted anywhere, but the Forester has a real PIN Code Access feature: with the fob out of range and ignition off, press the rear liftgate lock button in a sequence to register a 5-digit code (10 presses = "0", complete within 30 seconds), then re-enter that same sequence on the same button anytime to unlock without the fob. 5 wrong attempts locks it out for 5 minutes. Skip weak codes (00000, 12345, your plate digits) — the manual calls that out specifically.
  • EyeSight and the windshield are linked. A cracked windshield means OEM glass ($650-700) plus EyeSight camera recalibration ($220-600 depending on static vs. dynamic procedure) — commonly $800-1,200 all-in. Insist on OEM glass and a shop that explicitly does Subaru-spec calibration; cut-rate aftermarket glass can leave EyeSight silently miscalibrated.
  • Learn X-Mode (the AWD terrain-mode button near the shifter) and how to force a lower "gear" on the CVT for engine braking on long descents — exactly what you want for the mountain driving you're already doing.
  • The spare is a compact temporary only — no full-size option ever existed for this generation. Max 50 mph, use it on the rear only (swap a good rear wheel to the front if a front tire fails), inflate to 60 psi, and never fit tire chains to it. Find it under the cargo floor now and confirm the jack and tools are actually present.
  • No TPMS reset button exists on this generation. After a tire rotation the system self-relearns over ~10-15 minutes of driving above 25 mph once pressures are corrected. If a full wheel/tire set is ever replaced, a shop needs a TPMS tool to re-register the sensor IDs.

One thing that outranks this whole page: the paper maintenance-schedule booklet in the glovebox is model-year-specific and is the actual document Subaru issued for this exact car. If it disagrees with the table above, it wins.
