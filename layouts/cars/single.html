The verdict

At ordinary financing (7%+ new, ~9% used), the 2–3-year-old used car wins: past the depreciation cliff, still near the powertrain warranty, good-era CVT. Buying new that way costs ~$3.5–7k more over 7 years.

But the manufacturer APR changes the answer. With Subaru’s 1.9% / 36-month promo, a new Forester’s 7-year cost drops to ~$42k — the cheapest Forester line in the table, because saving ~7 points of interest more than offsets the extra depreciation. The catch is the payment: ~$906/mo for three years instead of ~$509 for the used one. A CPO Forester at ~4.29% splits the difference — ~$43k all-in, ~$486/mo, with warranty and clean history. See the incentives note below — those rates are real but move monthly and need confirming with a Boise ZIP.

Are CVTs bad?

They earned the reputation, then mostly fixed it — and Subaru’s history is worse than average.

  • Early CVTs (2010–2017) genuinely failed. Nissan’s Jatco units overheated and died by 60–90k miles; warranties were extended under lawsuit pressure.
  • Subaru’s TR580 (2014–2018 Forester/Outback/Impreza) shuddered and hesitated. The Yaeger v. Subaru class-action settlement extended the CVT warranty to 10 yr / 100,000 mi on those years — a tell that the base coverage wasn’t holding. Replacement cost when they go: $4,000–8,000.
  • The second-gen unit (2019+, TR580 revised + TR690) has a much better field record. Shudder complaints drop off sharply after 2019; no class action.
  • Honda’s CVT has been quietly reliable since ~2016. Toyota hedged with a physical launch gear, then dropped the CVT entirely on the 2019+ RAV4 (8-speed automatic).

Bottom line: a 2020–2023 Forester is a reasonable buy and the transmission shouldn’t keep you up at night — change the CVT fluid at 60k even though Subaru calls it “lifetime.” Want the worry gone entirely? That’s exactly what a 2019+ RAV4 or a 4Runner buys you.

The shortlist — camping + light offroading

The foothills use case (washboard gravel, forest two-track, ruts, snow — not rock crawling) rewards ground clearance and a lockable AWD/4WD mode. A road-biased crossover will scrape and spin.

PICK 1 — NO COMPROMISE

Toyota RAV4 TRD Off-Road (2019+)

No CVT · 8-sp auto8.6" clearanceRear-biased AWD + lock

Satisfies every constraint: Japanese, real gearbox, factory all-terrain tires, tuned dampers, torque-vectoring rear axle. ~$30–36k used. Class-leading resale.

PICK 2 — IF IT MUST BE A SUBARU

Subaru Forester Wilderness (2022+)

CVT (good era)9.2" clearanceDual-mode X-Mode + skid plate

The most dirt-capable Forester ever built — higher clearance than the RAV4, 700 lb roof load for a tent. It is a CVT, but the reliable one. ~$28–34k used.

PICK 3 — VALUE

Honda Passport (2019+)

No CVT · 9-sp auto8.1" clearancei-VTM4 AWD, dirt/snow modes

Underrated. Huge cargo box for camping, bulletproof Honda V6, cheaper than a 4Runner. Thirstier than the crossovers; modest approach angle.

PICK 4 — THE REAL OFFROADER

Toyota 4Runner (2010–2024, 5th gen)

No CVTBody-on-frameLocking rear diff (TRD)

Will outlast everything else and tow 5,000 lb. The objection is running cost: ~17 mpg, $623/mo all-in over 7 years, trucky on-road.

Drop for real dirt: the base (non-Wilderness) Forester — 8.7" clearance is fine for a snowy driveway and light gravel, but the road-tuned suspension and all-season tires give up quickly on a rutted forest road.

Toyota Certified Pre-Owned

Toyota’s CPO (TCUV) adds a 7yr/100k powertrain warranty from the original in-service date, a 12mo/12k comprehensive warranty, a 160-point inspection, and a guaranteed clean title — no rebuilt/branded cars can be certified. Worth a modest premium on a RAV4 (newer 8-speed auto); worth less on a 4Runner, whose drivetrain rarely fails — there you’re really buying the clean-history guarantee. Compare the out-the-door price delta vs. a comparable non-certified unit, not the badge.

Local Toyota dealer sites (Peterson, Steve’s Hometown) are JavaScript apps the scraper can’t read, and Larry H. Miller Toyota Boise has closed — so check dealer CPO inventory directly. Watch for fake badges: a “Certified” tag on a listing aggregator sometimes means a third-party warranty (e.g. “Ford Blue Advantage”), not genuine Toyota Certified. Confirm the listing says “Toyota Certified Used Vehicle, backed by Toyota.”

Test drive

Before you go

  • Ask for a cold car — arrange to arrive before it’s been started that day. A warmed-up engine hides the tells (startup rattle, smoke, rough idle).
  • Line up two or three in one day. Back-to-back is the only way to feel the difference — impressions fade fast.
  • She drives first and longest. She’s the primary driver — seat fit, visibility, driving position, and where the controls fall matter more than anything on paper.
  • Bring Grandpa (the mechanic) and an OBD2 Bluetooth reader ($20). Plug it in even with no warning light: pull stored/pending codes and check that the readiness monitors all say “ready” — several “not ready” means codes were recently cleared to drop a light before the sale.
  • Drive 25+ minutes: surface streets, a full-throttle highway on-ramp, rough pavement, and tight parking-lot circles both directions. Windows down the first few minutes to hear the drivetrain, then test the A/C.
  • CarMax offers a 24-hour test drive on most cars — use it for the Touring so she can live with it overnight.
  • Photograph everything: VIN, odometer, all four tire DOT date codes, any damage.

What you two drive for

  • Full-throttle merge, once. Any hesitation, rpm flare, or shudder is a flag.
  • Firm brake from 45 in a straight line — no pulling, no pedal pulsing (warped rotors), no ABS chatter on dry pavement.
  • Hold 65–70 on the highway — no steering-wheel vibration (wheels/balance/bent rim), no wandering (alignment), no wind roar (failed seal or prior bodywork).
  • Lift off the gas on a downhill — listen for a driveline clunk.
  • Every button: A/C blows genuinely cold, heat, all windows, sunroof, heated seats, CarPlay/screen, backup camera, all four door locks, cruise, wipers, rear defrost.
  • Nose test: musty = water intrusion (lift the floor mats and the spare-tire cover and feel for damp); sweet syrup smell = coolant leak.
  • Park on a hill, set the brake — confirm it actually holds.

Forester — the CVT drive

  • Accelerate smoothly 20 → 45 mph on light-to-moderate throttle. It should feel rubber-bandy but seamless. Any shudder, judder, or a “slipping / searching” sensation is the classic failing-CVT sign — walk away.
  • Stop-and-go creep: ease forward from a dead stop several times. Feel for a hesitation or a “catch” before it engages.
  • Engage X-Mode (button by the shifter) — confirm the dash indicator lights.
  • EyeSight on the highway: adaptive cruise engages and holds a gap; lane-keep nudges the wheel; no EyeSight fault light after startup.
  • Slight side-to-side shake at idle is normal for the boxer engine. A rhythmic miss or a top-end tick is not.

4Runner — the truck drive

  • The float-y, trucky ride is normal — don’t mistake it for worn shocks. But after a speed bump it should settle in one or two bounces, not keep bobbing.
  • Shift into 4-Hi (rolling slowly or stopped — it’s part-time on SR5/TRD): dash light confirms, no grinding. Shift back to 2WD and confirm no binding or clunk on pavement.
  • TRD trims: in a lot, test the locking rear diff (needs 4-Lo), Crawl Control, and Multi-Terrain Select — each should activate and indicate.
  • Power rear window (the glass that drops into the tailgate) — cycle it fully up and down. It’s a known, pricey failure point.
  • The 5-speed auto revs higher on the highway (~2,400 rpm at 75) — that’s the old gearbox, not a problem. Shifts should be smooth with no flare between gears.
  • Listen for a driveline vibration or drone at 40–50 mph (driveshaft / carrier bearing).

For Grandpa — the model-specific spots

He’ll know what a tired car feels like better than any list. These are the quirks specific to these models that might not come up if he hasn’t worked on them:

  • Forester — oil consumption. The FB25 boxer on some 2019–21 cars burns a quart per 1,000–1,200 mi. Check the dipstick cold; ask to come back in a week and recheck. Ask for oil-change records — short intervals suggest the owner was managing it.
  • Forester — CVT fluid. Subaru calls it “lifetime”; it isn’t. Has it ever been dropped and filled? Fluid should be reddish, not brown or burnt-smelling.
  • Forester — wheel bearings and rear lateral links. Droning hum that changes as you sway the car = bearing. Check the rear subframe and lateral-link bushings for rot (Idaho road salt).
  • Forester — windshield. Subaru glass plus EyeSight recalibration runs $1,000+. A chipped one is a bargaining chip.
  • 4Runner — frame rust. Less of an issue in dry Idaho than the salt belt, but still: probe the frame rails, especially around the rear spring hangers and the crossmember, and look for fresh undercoating hiding scale.
  • 4Runner — KDSS (if equipped): look for hydraulic fluid weeping at the valve body under the vehicle. A working system corners noticeably flatter.
  • 4Runner — the 1GR-FE V6 is chain-driven and famously tough; no known engine or 5-speed-auto failure modes. Front diff / CV boots and the secondary air-injection system (pre-2016) are the spots.
  • Both — tires: matched brand, even wear across all four. A mismatched set on an AWD/4WD car means neglect and possibly a chewed-up center diff or transfer case.
  • Both — CarFax / title: clean title, service at real intervals, one or two owners, no gap where it might have sat wrecked.
  • A lift inspection at an independent shop ($120–150) is still worth it even with Grandpa along — a second set of eyes and a rack you don’t have in a seller’s driveway.
